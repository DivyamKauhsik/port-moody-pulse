The Kid from East Hill Who Ran for Office: Wei Chou's Story

By Divyam Kaushik · 5 min read

It was a rainy afternoon when I met Wei Chou at a local cafe, the young queer candidate who ran in the provincial election for Port Moody - Burquitlam. Wei goes by he/him, they/them pronouns. We shook hands and sat down. Today, I got to listen to the 34-year-old tell their story from when they were five years old, and their family arrived in Port Moody from Taiwan in 1997. East Hill became home, and the new world felt like a puzzle. Mandarin stayed the language of home, while English became the language of community, and Saturday school taught Wei to write Chinese. The first years were scary and exciting in equal measure.

Sipping my coffee, Wei spoke about how the confusion lasted. In grade four, Wei's parents took the family back to Taiwan, and three years later, his family returned to Coquitlam, renting a basement on Westwood Plateau. Upon return, the young candidate remembered how fluent his Mandarin became, but he had to relearn English again. A year later, the family moved to South Surrey because his mom was concerned about the cannabis grow-ops spreading throughout the neighbourhood at the time.

It was in high school that Wei found his way into politics. In grade eleven, he sat as an observer on the student council and caught the grad council president try to take money out of Ice Cream Day to fund a graduation yacht trip. Ice Cream Day was meant for everybody. So Wei organized an occupation of the student government.

"That was my first foray into socialism," Wei joked. "Just fighting corruption from the ruling class."

After graduating with an economics degree, Wei went to work in supply chains. First, as a logistics coordinator for a long-haul trucking company, moving Montreal ice cream and California produce across Canada and the United States. Then at Landstar, a freight brokerage, procuring oversize trucking for things like huge HVAC generators. Finally, at Switchboard, a small fleet-management startup building devices that tracked driving hours and fuel efficiency, a company that went through Y Combinator in Silicon Valley. Wei had already tried starting a company once during university, Yilu Logistics, and it failed to take off. But the years working inside the industry was how the young entrepreneur planned to learn it properly before trying again.

In 2019, Wei returned to entrepreneurship and founded Cargo Sprout. The idea came from something that Wei had observed for years: most trucks in cities were running at 30 percent of capacity, the rest empty or deadheading. A sharing economy could match that unused space with shippers who needed it, an Airbnb for freight. The industry network that Wei made over the years became the first customers. Then the Pandemic nearly killed the business, and the company pivoted to a managed marketplace, using machine learning to read the rate-request emails shippers sent in and match them with trucking companies and warehouses. Containers moved from the Port of Vancouver to warehouses in Surrey, Calgary, Toronto, and Montreal. The platform transported everything from frozen seafood to Costco cheesecakes. By 2023, the company had made its first million.

A year later, Cargo Sprout was gone. The company had burned through about $2 million, much of it credit extended to customers on 30- and 60-day terms who never paid. Wei sat down with their co-founders, looked at the hard figures, and made the difficult call to wind the business down.

"I was not inclined to be one of those employers that go on the news for unpaid wages," claimed Wei, adding how he had to fight for unpaid wages when he was an employee. All their 14 employees got paid.

What followed was a hard year. The closure took a toll on Wei's mental health, and therapy followed. There were times when Wei depended on the support of food banks and community mental health services. Without them, the former candidate recalled, "how close I came to just being on the street." What brought Wei back was the community: family, friends, mentors, and a colleague from their university days. The floor that caught Wei was made of other people.

That floor is what pulled Wei back into politics. Around 2013, Wei had just joined the Federal Green Party for about a year, drawn by its environmental advocacy. Then came stints supporting the federal Liberals and the federal NDP, and then a drift away from politics entirely. The feeling was that nothing had changed, and that the parties were all kind of the same. Building the business occupied their interest instead.

The way back started with watching someone else step up. Emily Lowan, twenty-six, the youngest leader of a major party in Canada, had taken over the BC Greens and was touring the province on town halls, talking about a party that cared about community and social responsibility alongside the environment. Wei went to one of those town halls, then started going to community events, hearing what tenant unions and food-growing associations were struggling with, and joined the party. The decision to run came in January 2026. Then came the snap election, thirty days, an $80-million vote nobody had expected. "If I don't step up, who else is going to do it? If not now, then when?"

The choice partly came down to what a small party can do. The BC Greens have about 5,000 members and a staff of just over ten, but in minority governments the party has held the balance of power, and used it: banning political donations from corporations, unions, and out-of-province sources, bringing in benefit corporations, and passing laws that bar employers from forcing workers to wear high heels. The work Wei does now, as campaign manager and financial agent for two other Green candidates, runs on the same small-party culture: people helping each other out, volunteers doing the work, nobody too important to ask for help. Wei calls it one of the career diseases of entrepreneurship, a desire to take on every job, and a reminder to learn to delegate.

As the rain softened, I couldn't help but notice a through-line in all of it, and it runs through Port Moody. The city Wei remembers from childhood is a place of forests and water: walks with his mom in the woods, Scouts on East Hill from Beavers through Cubs, and one of the parents who was a Scout leader who took the kids out and showed them the trails. On camping trips, the rule from those scouting years still travels with Wei: to leave a place cleaner than when you came, and pick up two or three pieces of garbage, because somebody else might have dropped one. Rocky Point has, in Wei's judgement, one of the best ice creams anywhere. Brewer's Row, the Golden Spike Days festival, the fact that the transcontinental railway ended here before it extended to Vancouver; these are his childhood memories. What the 34-year-old former candidate values most about the city is simpler. You do not have to spend money to enjoy yourself here. A park, the water, the forest: that is the luxury.

Wei's dad, an architect, was one of the early members of the Green Building initiative in Taiwan, pushing for buildings that were sustainable and for green spaces in a country that was building condos faster than parks. The same instinct shows up in Wei's politics now: a country that extracts more than it creates, lumber and oil and water, cannot keep going that way, and the social contract, the deal where work earns a person housing and health and retirement, is fraying for the people who do the work.

The mask story sits in the middle of all of it. During COVID, Wei joined a volunteer group that went by "the Brigade", put together by a colleague in Toronto and some doctors who wanted to do something. The group found masks sitting unused in one health department and transferred them to another that was running out, 20,000 surgical masks in the end. Then they procured N95s and gowns from Paraguay, testing samples with doctors in real time. Wei had lived through SARS in Taiwan and knew what masks meant in a collective culture: you wear one so you do not make your co-workers sick. Then I heard Wei mention the profiteering they saw that disgusted them, hand sanitizer at forty dollars a bottle! And the Brigade charged nobody anything. Wei compares the spirit to the CCF, the party that grew out of mutual aid in Depression-era Saskatchewan and built universal health care. Community, outside the existing structures, rebuilding from the ground up.

The campaign this fall lasted 33 days and ended with 2,103 votes in Port Moody-Burquitlam, third place, on the certified final count. Wei is already planning the next one.

"I will be in for the next election," Wei quipped.

The reason is the same one that carried Wei through the company, the masks, and the year after: the floor that caught Wei was the community, and the way to pay that back is to step up.
