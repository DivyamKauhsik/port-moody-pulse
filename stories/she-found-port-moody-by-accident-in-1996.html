She Found Port Moody by Accident in 1996 and Never Left: Kyla Knowles's Story

By Divyam Kaushik · 6 min read

Kyla Knowles, re-elected to Port Moody council in 2026 with 4,119 votes, did not grow up in Port Moody. She grew up in Kitimat, a coastal town, and she first saw Port Moody the way a lot of people first see it: from the road, in passing, and then not again for years.

She moved down from Kitimat in the fall of 1994 to go to SFU, and lived in residence on Burnaby Mountain for four years. In the summer of 1996 she had a co-op job with what is now the Ministry of Social Development and Poverty Reduction. She was paired with another co-op student, and their job was to travel around the Lower Mainland doing home visits for case workers who did not have time to do them themselves. Two weeks each in New Westminster, Coquitlam, PoCo, Chilliwack, Abbotsford, Mission, and on. At the time she really only knew North Burnaby and downtown Vancouver, by bus. The job was her first real introduction to Metro Vancouver.

"It was during that job that I really 'discovered' Port Moody," she wrote. "It reminded me a lot of my coastal hometown with the mountains, trees and small town feel and I never forgot about it."

After she graduated, she moved to Vancouver and lived within bus or walking distance from the downtown financial district where she worked in corporate finance until 2013. She did not buy a car until her first daughter was born in 2012.

In 2013, pregnant with her second daughter, she was pushing her first child in a stroller across Main and 17th when a cyclist blew through a red light and nearly hit them. It scared her. All the conveniences of Vancouver that she had enjoyed for 13 years were no longer important to her as a mom, so she convinced her then-spouse they should look for a new home in Port Moody. It was time for the suburbs.

Knowing the SkyTrain was coming was the clincher. She wanted to buy before it arrived, because she knew it would push home prices up. They sold the condo on Main Street and put in an offer on the very first place they saw, a townhouse in Easthill.

She knew when they moved here in 2013 that she was here to stay. "It was immediately home, just as I knew it would be back in 1996, and I knew I'd be proud to raise my girls here."

She remembers being in the hospital after giving birth to her second daughter. The nurse asked for her address and she said they lived in Port Moody. The nurse looked at her and said, "Where is that? Is that out by Maple Ridge?" Kyla thought to herself, my god, what an amazing hidden gem. People who live in Vancouver cannot even find Port Moody on a map, and it is only 20 minutes from downtown.

"People here really, really care," she wrote. Her mom is often horrified by the things she and other public figures have had to deal with here, and Kyla always tells people it is because the people who live here really love it. What happens here matters. The feel of Port Moody and its uniqueness matters.

And families are generational. Both her girls went to Glenayre Elementary, and she remembers meeting other parents there who told her they also went to Glenayre. Staying local in a small city like Port Moody, when there are so many other communities to live in, really says something, she thinks.

She had always followed and enjoyed politics, but she was not terribly interested in the municipal level. It seemed boring. And honestly, she says, maybe it should be.

She became very invested in the 2018 election because it suddenly was not boring at all, and she felt a lot was at stake. The results were not what she had hoped, and things got worse from there. That term, she says, put Port Moody on the map in the worst kind of way, with no code of conduct for elected officials, protests at City Hall, and what she felt was misogyny and poor treatment of City staff. She went to the protests, signed petitions, and wrote letters to the editor.

Then in 2021, a good friend asked her if she would consider running in the 2022 election. Her first reaction was hell no. In her regular life she can be very blunt, and she did not want people prying into her private life. She promised to think about it anyway.

She had been dating her current spouse for two years when she told him she was thinking about running. By that time, he knew that when she brings up an "idea" to him, it is probably fully formed. Her mom, who still lives up north, thought she should wait until her girls were older. But Kyla felt this was the time. Being a mom of two girls was the additional push she needed. Her girls, who were 8 and 10 at the time, did not really understand what she was doing. They sure do now.

Asked about the new Kyle Centre, she is excited about the plan for a new community centre to replace the aging one at 125 Kyle Street, growing from 11,000 square feet to a proposed 39,000, with new neighbourhood parkland bought using the city's parkland reserve. "Our community and residents deserve a new recreation centre, not small patches on a crumbling building," she wrote. She is looking forward to it opening, and to what it will look like with the new, expanded footprint, outside sports and art, and a great new neighbourhood park many times the size of the current one.

Asked for one housing story from her time on council, the story she reached for was not about a house at all. It was about the term's hardest stretch: council agreeing, by majority vote, to involve itself in matters outside its jurisdiction, and the division, arguing, protests, and threats that followed. It reminded her of the 2018-2022 term, in the worst possible way, she wrote. She believes strongly that a city council has enough work to do in its own lane. She and Councillor Morrison brought forward a motion to make it more difficult for council to involve itself in non-jurisdictional matters going forward. It passed 5-2, and she hopes current and future councils keep it that way.

This term, she says, she is going to push for improved financial accountability and spending, transparency, and assisting local businesses and homeowners when they come to City Hall. Her main focus last term was getting the city back on track in terms of reputation, good governance, relationships, and finance, and she feels they achieved that, and then some. She wants to make sure that is not lost, and that council does not backslide into long, late night meetings with tons of staff overtime and consultants.

She lives by an adage a former mayor of the city gave her: "Don't take yourself seriously, but take the job seriously." It has got her through some difficult attacks, she says. She takes the job very seriously.

She gives that one to Councillor Callan Morrison. They met on the campaign trail in 2022, and after they were both elected they were seated next to each other at the table. They kind of figured out this whole council thing together. They do not always agree or vote the same, but they are brutally honest with each other and bicker like siblings. This term they have been seated on very opposite ends of the table, but she says it does not really matter. Callan and his wife Jessica also have two daughters, and it has been a real joy becoming close to their family. She has City Council to thank for that.

One of the things she finds most interesting about Port Moody, and always has, is that while people always think they might not want change, they usually end up loving it. That goes for the SkyTrain, Newport Village, Suterbrook, and Klahanie, all things people once protested and fought against, now beloved and part of the city fabric.

She remembers driving through Port Moody back in the 90s and thinking it was a great place to live except for St. Johns, which badly needed a refresh and revitalization. Yet here we are, 30 years later, and St. Johns still has not changed much. She envisions a much brighter, walkable Moody Centre, with more retail, vibrant streetscapes and third spaces, and neighbours interacting at community gardens and dog parks. Right now St. Johns feels like just a traffic thoroughfare. It will take commitment and work by council to change that, and she says she is so ready.

If she is being honest, it felt bittersweet, because she felt the city lost some really incredible women in the election. She was obviously happy that her hard work over the last four years had been recognized, particularly since she has a very clear and consistent viewpoint when it comes to governance and the future of Port Moody. It feels great knowing that so many voters share her viewpoint and priorities.

What she will most remember is her daughter walking down the stairs to hug her and saying "congratulations". At 14 now, her daughter gets what a big deal it is to win when you are running against 15 other candidates.
