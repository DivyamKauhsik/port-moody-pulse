Haven Lurbiecki

Running for Mayor of Port Moody
ChallengerPort Moody Voice team
✓ Verified by the candidate
Why are you running for mayor?
Lurbiecki is motivated by residents who are concerned about the direction of the city — residents tell her "this isn't what I thought I was voting for." Lurbiecki wants to bring change to the council table with the Port Moody Voice team, committed to accountability and a vision that comes from the community.
What's one issue that you believe matters most to residents in Port Moody?
Development — the plan for 14 towers up to 40 stories in Moody Centre, and its impacts on traffic and on parks not keeping up, are top of mind for residents.
If elected, what are your top three priorities for Port Moody?
First, trust and accountability at city hall: limit in-camera meetings and make the platform the strategic plan with public reporting. Second, redo the community plan so it represents what the community actually needs — family housing, seniors housing, affordable housing. Third, manage the impacts of growth and pay for them: infrastructure must keep pace; Lurbiecki was the only council vote against delaying higher infrastructure levies.
How do we balance growth and protecting Port Moody's character?
Fourteen towers up to 40 stories in four square blocks around the SkyTrain station can't avoid changing Port Moody; no one Lurbiecki speaks to opposes growth, but it must be balanced. The city needs its plans, bylaws, and affordable-housing policies in place so council can reject proposals that don't meet community needs. Lurbiecki rejects the "NIMBY" label, noting a record of supporting developments that met the community's needs.

Meghan Lahti

Running for Mayor of Port Moody
Incumbent
✓ Verified by the candidate
What motivated you to run for a second term as mayor? Tell us why you're running again.
Lahti has been mayor for one term after many years on council (first elected in 1996), and says there's still a lot Lahti wants to accomplish — the job isn't finished for Lahti yet.
What's one issue that you would like to see addressed?
The economy and responsible spending — everyone is feeling it at the pump and the grocery store, and residents want the city to be responsible with property-tax dollars.
What would you say that the top three priorities would be for Port Moody?
First, diversify and expand the tax base — bring in business and industry to add stable tax dollars. Second, plan amenities for a growing city, starting with the Kyle Centre, plus a new park integrated with the arts centre. Third, get a handle on congestion on St. Johns Street.
Housing seems to be a major concern. How do you balance protecting Port Moody's character?
Lahti has long been committed to maintaining Port Moody's heritage character and charm. Every decision Lahti makes is people-based and centered on what the community as a whole will benefit from — Lahti's not interested in growth for growth's sake. Lahti notes Newport Village and Suter Brook were contentious too, and are now where most residents live.
What are some of your thoughts on density and infrastructure keeping pace with growth?
Infrastructure like hospitals and schools is provincial responsibility, and Lahti finds it frustrating that the province mandates growth — allowing up to four units on single-family lots and eight-storey entitlements in parts of Moody Centre — while withdrawing funds for affordable housing, hospital infrastructure, and at-capacity schools. The city is doing what it can under pressure from both sides.
In one minute or less, why should residents vote for you?
Lahti brings experience, context, passion, and a love for the community evident in everything Lahti does; Lahti has worked hard and will continue to work hard, believing every person should feel heard — from morning Lahti thinks about how to make Port Moody a better place to live and how to serve the community better.

Alexandra Chan

Running for Port Moody City Council
ChallengerPort Moody Voice slate
✓ Verified by the candidate
Alexandra, why are you running for Port Moody Council?
A year or so ago Chan started hearing about the OCP, went to council and a public hearing, and felt nothing residents said was being taken into account — the decision felt pre-made. Chan met Haven, shared values about communication, got involved as Port Moody Voice started, and agreed to run.
What's one issue you believe matters most to Port Moody residents right now?
Broadly, the future: climate, two-metre ocean-level rise, food security, affordability of where people and their children will live, whether seniors can stay — and whether the community can stay healthy, since without health a good quality of life is hard.
If elected, what are your top three priorities for Port Moody?
First, with the Port Moody Voice team, a culture of open communication and transparency — involve the public as much as possible in a trusting, close relationship with no hidden cards. Second, health: as a nurse Chan believes the best healthcare is prevention — cities can engineer walkability, less traffic, better sleep, decreasing chronic-illness risk factors. Third, reduce burdens on people: traffic, keeping the small-town feeling as the city grows, and investing in businesses and the commercial district so people can work here with real careers, honouring the industrial heritage.
Housing is a major concern. How do we balance growth, affordability, and protecting Port Moody's character?
Look outside the market: saturating the market with market-rate housing doesn't meaningfully change affordability — the city needs non-market and below-market housing, e.g. subsidized housing for frontline workers like nurses, firefighters, and police, as other countries do. And don't only build housing: property taxes rose 28% in four years partly because Port Moody is becoming a bedroom community — business support keeps the cost of living steady.
What are your thoughts on density, development, and infrastructure keeping pace with growth?
Chan agrees with growth, provided that infrastructure and services don't lag and impact quality of life. It's important developments contribute adequately to the community, and stay true to the city's character. Chan would like to see more homes and neighbourhoods designed with health outcomes in mind and stronger consideration for emergency preparedness.
In under one minute, why should residents vote for you?
Chan's an authentic person — life's too short for pretense. As a nurse and neighbour, the goal is investing in the future, alleviating worries, and working together toward a stronger and brighter future.

Amy Lubik

Running for Port Moody City Council
Incumbent
✓ Verified by the candidate
Why are you running again for the city of Port Moody?
The city has accomplished a lot — growing the tree canopy, working on affordable housing, expanding parks — but there is more to do: deepen affordability, protect the environment and neighbours, and focus on community health, from neighbourhood design to health infrastructure like family practices.
What's one issue that you believe matters most to Port Moody residents right now?
Affordability — over 600 units of affordable housing recently completed or in the pipeline, and many new purpose-built rentals are bringing down rents across the province. There are also opportunities to connect people with the rent bank to prevent homelessness, and to protect older rentals that are affordable due to age through the rental protection fund.
If reelected, what are your top three priorities for Port Moody?
Healthy community, affordable housing, and climate resilience. Lubik previously worked at the BC Centre for Disease Control on how to support cities in climate resilience and social connectedness.
How are we going to balance growth and affordability, all at the same time as protecting Port Moody's little quaint character?
Keep the connective character of Newport and Suter Brook in new neighbourhoods: third spaces like coffee shops, park spaces, tree canopy, and design guidelines; the Moody Centre framework is designed to capture what the city wants while bringing economic development and enlivening the public realm; community programming that brings people together is part of keeping the feel.
What are your thoughts on density, development, and infrastructure keeping pace with the growth of the city?
Density can be done well or poorly — negotiate multifunctional communities with affordable housing, commercial space, and parks. On social infrastructure: Lubik and Mayor Lahti gave notice on a motion to find more doctor spaces, they're talking with Fraser Health about health anchor tenants, petitioning the school district on schools, and including daycare in new developments (about 1,600 new spaces in 8 years, 500+ in the works). On parks: more parcels for Kyle Centre and Rocky Point expansion in the OCP. Green infrastructure prolongs pipe life. On traffic: St. Johns redesigns for active transportation and transit, which is harder since roads can't expand much more.
In 1 minute or less, why should voters vote for you again?
Lubik keeps residents' health at the forefront of decision-making: as a healthcare professional who moved from cancer research into public health, Lubik believes housing, green space, and community connectedness are what keep people healthy and thriving — not just the absence of disease.

Callan Morrison

Running for Port Moody City Council
Incumbent
Pending verification
Why are you running for Port Moody Council again?
Morrison became a father during this term and calls it literally the best job ever had — community involvement, events, communicating with residents about the future, and making positive change. So long here and so invested, Morrison wants to continue the good work.
What's one issue you believe matters most to Port Moody residents right now?
People wanting their quality of life to improve as the community evolves. Growth puts strain — construction, traffic — but done right and planned sequentially, controversial projects become beloved communities, as Newport Village and Suter Brook did.
If you're reelected, what are your top three priorities for Port Moody?
First, traffic mitigation: Morrison brought the motion to widen the CPKC overpass (completing next year), increasing Moody–Suter Brook flow by about 40% at low cost without sacrificing active transportation. Second, accessibility of recreation programs — from transit to the rec centre site, which is hard even with a stroller. Third, long-term planning: a sequential plan for the pool, Kyle Centre, and library, since priorities compete and money isn't unlimited.
How do we balance growth, affordability, and protecting Port Moody's character?
The distinct neighbourhoods make a major character change unlikely — people make the character. The province's housing bills mid-OCP forced 8/12/20-storey minimums near SkyTrain, and the resulting land lift made family housing harder; the city responded with minimum unit sizes based on BC Housing, restricted studios, and required minimum two-, three-, and three-plus-bedroom units — for a well-planned community welcoming all income levels and housing types.
What are your thoughts on density, development, and infrastructure and keeping pace with growth?
Housing bills designated higher density where the city had planned extra parkland, making park acquisition costlier through land lift, and infrastructure wasn't built for high density. Manage it with calculated planning and strong advocacy for a citywide planning process with resident input — "you can't just slap down one rule and make it applicable to every municipality."
In one minute, why should residents vote for you?
Morrison's delivered on deliverable promises: meaningful steps including early resident access to recreation before other cities, traffic mitigation, and supporting colleagues' initiatives. Morrison would continue fiscally responsibly, benefiting the most people, with collaboration at the table.

Diana Dilworth

Running for Port Moody City Council
Incumbent
✓ Verified by the candidate
Tell us why you're running for Port Moody Council again.
Dilworth loves Port Moody and its community. A resident since 1991 who raised a family here, Dilworth points to a proven record of making good decisions and wants to keep going. Dilworth has seen positive change since the days when Klahani was a steel company, Suter Brook was a forest, and Newport Village had just one tower.
What's one issue that you believe matters most to Port Moody residents right now?
Growth, density, traffic, and transportation — the same concerns as 20–30 years ago; it's about evolving to that change.
If reelected, what are your top three priorities for Port Moody?
First, parks: keep expanding park space, playgrounds, and trails in neighbourhoods across the community — Westhill Park and pop-up parks — not just Rocky Point. Second, housing: solid plans for the full spectrum — seniors, students, singles, couples, families — maximizing existing land through laneway and infill housing. Third, seniors: more opportunities and activities, including a standalone senior centre and gathering spaces like a new library.
How do we balance growth, affordability, and protecting Port Moody's character?
Look to Newport, Klahani, and Suter Brook — 25–30 years of planning created thriving, vibrant communities — and make sure new neighbourhoods like Inlet Centre and Moody Centre have that same charm and balance.
What are your thoughts on density, development, and infrastructure keeping pace with growth?
Dilworth's first campaign brochure promised to build infrastructure before accepting more growth; "growth pays for growth" through development and amenity cost charges — developers pay millions to tens of millions toward roads, sewers, and parks — and new facilities are impossible without growth because the city relies on homebuilders.
Tell me why residents should vote for you again.
Dilworth's life has been dedicated to public service — school boards, kids' activities, community, and dozens of volunteer boards — and Dilworth believes Dilworth's track record shows good, common-sense decisions that benefit the residential and business community.

Jeanine Longley

Running for Port Moody City Council
Challenger
✓ Verified by the candidate
Tell us why you're running.
Longley felt a sense of urgency after watching news about climate change and feeling the affordability crisis at the grocery store - she realized she had to act. Municipal politics felt like an opportunity to make a day-to-day difference for her neighbours, with a ripple effect outward on bigger problems.
What's the one issue that you believe matters most to Port Moody residents?
The gap between our growth and what we need in the city: people worry the city won't close the gap and make sure everyone has the amenities, services, and infrastructure they need.
If elected, what are your top three priorities for Port Moody?
Climate is what inspired Longley - and Port Moody is already a leader in this area. Secondly - protecting the charm and character of Port Moody, rooted in small businesses and local business owners. Third, turning down the heat on polarized discourse and finding a middle path of reasonable conversation.
How do we balance growth, affordability, and also protect Port Moody's character?
Affordability is really important, and often there is an explicit trade where higher density can bring the affordable or family friendly housing we desperately need - in these cases it is worth it. Character is connection and unique places - built through both the commercial and natural landscape, and how the community acts together.
What are your thoughts on density, development, and infrastructure keeping pace with growth?
It's a prioritization challenge. As a director of product management, Longley's career has included taking stakeholders with very different priorities and building one agreed upon plan - she wants to similarly align the community on what to prioritize, what to spend, and how much to grow.
Why should residents vote for you?
Longley brings career skills — negotiating many different priorities into one clearly communicated plan, which Longley says is what Port Moody needs right now — and hopes to earn everyone's vote on October 17.

Kyla Knowles

Running for Port Moody City Council
Incumbent
✓ Verified by the candidate
Why do you want to run for reelection?
Knowles says this council has achieved a lot in four years — a safer and healthier community, doubled the number of affordable housing units in Port Moody in only four years, traffic work underway, a greenlit Kyle Centre, Moody Centre parkland and tree canopy, and hundreds of new daycare spaces — and Knowles wants to continue that work rather than go backwards.
What's one issue that you believe matters most to Port Moody residents?
Traffic, which has been a perennial issue, and property rights — residents are concerned about losing control over what they can do on their property, dating back to the ESA issue and renewed by provincial uncertainty around title; Knowles says the municipality must be proactive in assuring residents their properties are theirs.
If reelected, what would your top three priorities be?
First, get the Kyle Centre built. Second, a new library — Knowles now supports it after hearing the library board's research — possibly on the old firehall lands, perhaps mixed with below-market seniors housing. Third, continue diversifying city revenues to take the burden off taxpayers.
How do we balance growth and affordability and also protect Port Moody's character?
Negotiate with community builders and stakeholders in good faith — delays add costs that fall on residents — and push back on the province's Bill 44, which lets 99% of single-family lots build three to six units and Knowles calls potentially devastating for Port Moody's character; also advocate to the federal government for funding. Knowles looks forward to Moody Centre becoming a vibrant, walkable neighbourhood with its amenities.

Knowles hasn't missed a single council meeting in four years — the only councillor who can say that — and voted against the 2023 council pay increase. Laura Soucy

Running for Port Moody City Council
ChallengerPort Moody Voice slate
✓ Verified by the candidate
Why are you running for city council?
Soucy grew up in Port Moody and raised a son here as a single parent. With wages too low for many residents to afford living here, Soucy wants local children to have the same opportunity to stay.
What is one issue you believe matters most to Port Moody residents right now?
Approved developments aren't delivering the family, seniors' and affordable housing the city's Housing Needs Report identifies as a shortfall. Soucy wants council partnering with non-profits, community land trusts and housing co-ops to build those forms of housing.
If elected, what are your top three priorities for Port Moody?
Repeal the 14-tower Moody Centre framework, with towers up to 40 storeys, and remake the area as an economic and business hub: anchor employers broadening the tax base so homeowners carry less of the budget, on a site served by both SkyTrain and the West Coast Express. Direct development to documented needs, weigh traffic, infrastructure and amenities with every approval, and press other governments on school and hospital space.
How do we balance growth, affordability, and protecting Port Moody's character?
Hold neighbourhood development to existing heritage and character: respect height limits, enforce setbacks, and follow the city's Housing Policy on every approval.
What are your thoughts on density, development, and infrastructure keeping pace with growth?
Growth should pay for growth, including the infrastructure to support it, but Soucy says that isn't happening: upgrades are already behind and developments are approved with no plan in place. Council needs to weigh all aspects of every approval, and where growth comes from provincial requirements, push back for the facilities to support it. The city doesn't manage schools or health care, but it approves the rate of population growth, so the time to secure school and health care space is when developments are approved, working in partnership with the responsible governments. Density has its place in transit-oriented areas, provided the planned housing is actually needed, a need the city's Housing Reports can establish.
Why should residents vote for you?
Bringing representation to Soucy's side of the city and lifting engagement: low turnout reflects a lack of connection. Soucy offers collaborative, balanced decision-making and more diverse, affordable housing so all families can call Port Moody home.

Tell our viewers why you're running for Port Moody Council.
Kunzli believes in doing a good job and bringing a lot to the table — raising two daughters in Port Moody, and wanting better discussions and better community outcomes. A council meeting a year and a half ago, attended with no intention of running, ended with people asking Kunzli to run.

Paul Lambert

Running for Port Moody City Council
Challenger
Pending verification
Why are you running for Port Moody Council?
I started this campaign running to be the next Mayor of Port Moody, and the response from the community through May, June and July was very positive. Then in August, my father had a heart attack and tragically passed away. After a lot of thought, I decided to run for City Council instead — campaigning for Mayor is very intense, and I need to put my family first and be there for my mother and my three young kids. Running for Council lets me keep my commitment to bring positive change to Port Moody, and I am very excited about the opportunity to serve our community on Council.
What is one issue you believe matters most to Port Moody residents?
Finding the right level of development and population growth. I'm running to offer a middle ground — we need to grow enough to address our housing needs and grow our local economy, but not so much that our infrastructure is overwhelmed and we risk losing what makes Port Moody so special. To stay unbiased on this issue, I have chosen to not take donations from real estate developers.
If you are elected, what are your top three priorities for Port Moody?
1) Find the middle ground on development and population growth. 2) Significantly upgrade our road infrastructure to finally improve traffic in Port Moody. 3) Put an end to the large property tax and utility fee increases residents have seen the past four years.
Housing is a major concern. How do we balance growth, affordability, and protect Port Moody's character?
Since 2015, housing has been the #1 issue in Port Moody and the Lower Mainland. The good news is that housing prices and rents are decreasing for the first time in 10 years, which has eased the pressure to add density — I believe this gives voters a choice to change course to a more reasonable level of growth. My plan: build fewer small condos and more larger units and townhouses for families, young people starting families, and seniors looking to downsize.
What are your thoughts on density, development, infrastructure, and keeping up with the pace?
The majority of residents I talk to feel Port Moody is out of balance — we have grown too much the past 10 years and our infrastructure has not kept up: our road network, public pools, and the emergency room at Eagle Ridge Hospital. My solution: 1) slow development and growth to a reasonable level — I'm not anti-development, but we have to strike the right balance; 2) upgrade our infrastructure — roads, current pools, new pickleball courts, planning for our first indoor pool, and advocating for the Province to expand Eagle Ridge Hospital.
Why should people vote for you?
Residents have been clear they are looking for change, and I believe my platform represents that change. I am not anti-development, but we should slow the pace of growth and focus on larger units and townhouses for families and for seniors to downsize to. I do not take donations from real estate developers, so I remain unbiased. We need infrastructure upgrades, especially roads, and I will oppose large property tax increases. It would be an honour to earn your vote on October 17th.

Reece Mack

Running for Port Moody City Council
ChallengerPort Moody Voice
✓ Verified by the candidate
Tell us why you're running for Port Moody City Council.
Mack is concerned about the tone of civic discourse and says residents don't feel heard by the current council; Mack wants "the residents put back in the center of the conversation of every decision that's made in city hall."
What is one issue you believe matters most to Port Moody residents right now?
Mack sees the election as "a referendum on the future of the city," citing the Moody Centre 14-tower plan — which Mack says 70% of residents rejected in engagement yet is moving ahead — as a clear example of people not being heard.
What are your top three priorities if elected?
First, repeal the 14-tower plan and instead develop a business district, to bring businesses back and ease the property-tax burden on homeowners. Second, improve traffic flow and infrastructure planning — including exploring tools like Google's Project Greenlight — so infrastructure keeps pace with the OCP growth number. Third, fund PFAS-free bunker gear for firefighters, noting firefighter occupational cancer is the leading cause of death among firefighters.
How do we balance growth, affordability, and protecting Port Moody's character?
Growth should meet community needs rather than investor demand — Mack favours 3- to 5-bedroom townhouses and rowhouses over studio-to-one-bedroom units, plus co-ops and below-market options; Mack wants new builds to honour Port Moody's policy of 10–15% below-market rental, which Mack says is "seldom used these days."
What are your thoughts on density, development, and infrastructure keeping pace with growth?
Growth is good but "it's how we grow" that matters; expensive capital projects like sewer and water must be in the plan from the start and keep pace with whatever growth number the OCP sets.
Why should residents vote for you?
Mack cares about the city and its people, sees elected officials as public servants holding "guardianship with care," and Mack's most important message is that everyone should go vote on October 17.

Tell us why you're running for Port Moody City Council in this election. Why now?
A lifelong Port Moody resident of 27 years, Moon says Moon felt Moon's voice wasn't heard, and is motivated by affordability, traffic, population growth, and wildlife concerns. Moon built Moon's community, family, and business in Port Moody and wants to give back.
What is one issue you believe matters most to Port Moody residents right now?
Property taxes — residents fund a large share of city costs through property taxes, which keep rising. Moon says the OCP's 74,000-people-by-2050 projection theoretically lowers costs but ignores the root problem: sustainable growth plus businesses and tech contributing to a stable economy.
If elected, what are your top three priorities for Port Moody?
Revise the Official Community Plan — Moon proposes planning for about 55,000 people instead of 74,300, matching what infrastructure, green space, traffic, roads, recreation, schools, primary care, and hospitals can handle, with a balance of mid- and low-rise development plus a few high-rises rather than all towers.
Housing is a major concern. How do we balance growth, affordability, and protecting Port Moody's character?
Bring in businesses that generate revenue and pay taxes so the burden doesn't fall on residents — and possibly lower development cost charges to make family housing more affordable. Rising property taxes must not push elders and longtime residents out.
What are your thoughts on density, development, and infrastructure keeping pace with growth?
Don't grow so fast the city can't handle it — be prepared. Green space and wildlife must be protected: Moon notes more than 100 bird species migrate from the inlet and 14 towers on St. Johns would block that; growth must be sustainable.
Why should people vote for you on October 17th?
For people who want their voices heard, especially the younger generation — on growth moving too fast, financial and ecological planning, and lowering property taxes so the community can thrive.

Tell us why you're running for school trustee.
Morrison went through SD43 from elementary to high school and is a Port Moody resident whose two young children — in kindergarten and Grade 2 — will spend their whole schooling in the district. Morrison believes schools in Port Moody and across the district should be places where children feel safe, supported, and able to thrive, with strong, present, and consistent local representation at the board table.
What do you see as the biggest challenge facing students in SD43 today?
Student safety and support — a concern Morrison hears from parents and students alike. The youth mental-health situation is the biggest crisis in memory and sits at the base of student safety; more support is needed, and the district hiring 50 new EAs is a step. Having been through a high-school lockdown, Morrison calls them terrifying and says they aren't always fully communicated.
If you are elected, what would be your top three priorities during your first term as a trustee?
Safe and supported schools: students cannot learn properly in overcrowded, underfunded schools where teachers cannot fully do their jobs. Planning for growth: Port Moody's student population is estimated to rise 25%, urban schools are already at capacity, and facilities must be built and ready before the growth arrives. And full, dedicated representation — Morrison commits to being at everything and listening to the community.
School districts face competing financial pressures. How would you approach a budget decision when faced with difficult choices?
Advocacy first. SD43 is the third-largest district in B.C. but sits in the bottom 10 for per-student funding — almost $1,000 per student in funding the district does not receive — so trustees must be the 'squeaky wheel' to the province and exhaust every avenue before cutting programs, supports, EAs, or teachers. Budget decisions also require collaboration among the nine trustees, and Morrison would ensure Port Moody gets its fair share.
What role should schools play in supporting mental health and well-being?
Notice early and follow through. Morrison was bullied badly through elementary and middle school — badly enough to switch schools in Grade 8 — and says it is harder now, when bullying follows students home online around the clock. Not having the supports needed as a student is a driving force for Morrison's advocacy for those support systems in the district.
Why should voters choose to vote for you for school trustee in Port Moody?
Morrison will be the strongest advocate for fair funding and future planning, and is committed to residents, their kids, and the community. Morrison has openly committed to serving three terms — this is not a stepping stone to a different office — out of genuine care for Port Moody's school system, community, and kids.
